Oven Belt Specification

How to measure a balanced weave belt and how to identify CB and Z47 oven belts before ordering a replacement.

Why Correct Specification Matters

Oven belts that look alike at first glance can have very different constructions. A small difference in spiral count, wire diameter or weave type changes strength, weight, open area, heat transfer and the way the belt behaves on the drums.

A replacement belt specified from incomplete measurements may not track correctly in the oven, may mark the product differently, or may require tension and tracking adjustments that the original belt never needed. The goal of specification is therefore to identify the weave model and record the measurements that define it.

Tools and Preparation

Recommended tools

  • Caliper (preferably digital)
  • Outside micrometer for wire diameter
  • Steel ruler or tape measure graduated in millimeters and inches
  • Magnifier or camera with macro function
  • Marker or industrial chalk to mark the counting area
  • Camera for photos with a ruler placed next to the belt

Belt preparation

  • Take measurements with the oven switched off, cold, locked out and with safety procedures applied
  • Clean the measuring area to remove product residue and carbon buildup
  • Choose representative areas, away from splices, repairs, damaged edges or deformed sections
  • Repeat each measurement in at least three locations across the width and along the length
  • If a loose sample of the belt is available, use it: it allows more accurate measurements and photos from every side
Reference length: 12 inches

Balanced and CB belt designations use counts per 12 inches, that is, per 304.8 mm. When counting in millimeters, always use this reference length so the result matches the belt designation.

How to Measure a Balanced Weave Belt

A balanced weave belt is made of wire spirals wound alternately right-hand and left-hand. Each pair of spirals is joined by a cross rod called a connector. Alternating the spiral direction balances the lateral forces so the belt tends to run straight. The result is a closed, flat and strong surface widely used in ovens.

How to recognize it

  • Adjacent spirals with alternating winding direction
  • One connector running through each pair of spirals across the full width
  • Connectors usually crimped, keeping the spiral loops evenly spaced
  • A single layer of weave, with visible openings between spirals and connectors

How to read the designation

The designation of a balanced weave belt follows the format B XX-YY-ZZ:

BBalanced weave
XXSpiral loops per 12" of width
YYConnectors per 12" of length
ZZWire gauge

When the gauge has four digits, the first two indicate the connector gauge and the last two the spiral gauge. Suffixes may indicate additional features, such as F for flattened wire and M for metric construction.

Step 1 — Loop count across the width (XX)

  1. Mark a 304.8 mm (12") section across the belt width, perpendicular to the direction of travel.
  2. Count the spiral loops within that section, along a single connector.
  3. Repeat in at least three positions and use the most frequent value.

If the belt is narrow or the count is high, count over 152.4 mm (6") and multiply by 2, but confirm the result with a second count.

Step 2 — Connector count along the length (YY)

  1. Mark a 304.8 mm (12") section in the direction of travel.
  2. Count how many connectors cross that section.
  3. Repeat at different points along the length, away from the splice.
Pitch calculation

Lateral pitch = 304.8 mm ÷ XX

Longitudinal pitch = 304.8 mm ÷ YY

Measuring the pitch directly over 10 loops or 10 connectors and dividing by 10 is a good way to check the count.

Step 3 — Wire diameter (ZZ)

Use a micrometer to measure the spiral wire diameter and the connector diameter separately. Record the value in millimeters and compare it with the gauge table below, choosing the closest gauge. On flattened wire, also record the width and thickness of the flattened profile.

Step 4 — Other measurements

  • Overall width: edge to edge, including the edge finish
  • Overall thickness: with a caliper, at several points across the width
  • Overall length: measured with the belt under operating tension, or calculated from the oven belt path
  • Edge finish: welded or looped
  • Material: stainless steel or carbon steel, according to the original belt records
  • Direction of travel and product-contact side

How to Identify a CB Belt

A CB (compound balanced) belt is made of two to five balanced belts interlocked and compressed into a single dense belt. The result is a very closed, smooth surface with the smallest openings of any woven wire belt. It is therefore widely used for products that need uniform support and even heat distribution.

How to recognize it

  • Very dense surface, with almost no openings visible to the naked eye
  • Several connectors passing through the same spiral loop
  • Spirals nested into one another, appearing as several layers of weave
  • Much higher weight per square meter than a balanced belt of the same width
  • Edges usually welded

How to determine the number after CB

The number after the letters indicates how many belts make up the weave. A CB3 belt has three connectors running through each spiral; a CB5, five.

  1. Look at the belt edge or a cut sample, where the connectors are easiest to see.
  2. Choose a single spiral loop and count how many connectors pass through it.
  3. Repeat on other loops to confirm. The number found corresponds to the number after CB.

How to read the designation

The designation follows the same principle as the balanced weave belt, in the format CBn XX-YY-ZZZZ:

CBnCompound balanced weave with n component belts
XXSpiral loops per 12" of width
YYConnectors per 12" of length
ZZZZConnector gauge followed by spiral gauge
Reading example

CB5-27-84-1416F — compound weave of five belts, 27 loops per 12" of width, 84 connectors per 12" of length, 14-gauge connector, 16-gauge spiral and flattened wire (F).

CB-specific measurements

  • Connector count: since the count is high, count over 76.2 mm (3") or 152.4 mm (6") and multiply, repeating at different points
  • Loop count: count along a single connector, as on the balanced weave belt
  • Flattened wire: check whether the top face of the wire is flat; if so, record the width and thickness of the profile
  • Ground surface: check whether the product-contact face has been ground flat
  • Overall thickness and weight per square meter: help distinguish constructions with similar counts

How to Identify a Z47 Belt

The Z47 is a rolled belt for tunnel ovens. Unlike balanced and CB belts, it is made only of round wire spirals, with no cross rods. The spirals have alternating winding direction and are interlocked in pairs with the adjacent spirals. After weaving, the mesh is rolled to obtain a flat, smooth surface, cut to width, and the wires are spot welded at the edges.

Since there are no connectors to absorb tension, the tension is distributed across the full width of the mesh. This requires very regular spirals and makes pitch and wire-diameter measurements especially important.

How to recognize it

  • No connectors or cross rods
  • Only spirals interlocked with one another along the full length
  • Flat surface on both sides, as a result of rolling
  • Wires with a slightly flattened appearance on the top and bottom faces
  • Wires spot welded at the edges
  • Splice made through the mesh itself, without adapter segments

Dimensional reference

The Z47 corresponds to the construction with a wire diameter of approximately 1.2 mm and a mesh opening of approximately 4 mm, also referred to as type 40/12. Other rolled belts use different combinations of opening and wire, so the measurements below are needed to confirm the model.

Z47-specific measurements

  1. Wire diameter: measure on the side of the wire, in the direction that was not rolled. Confirm that the value is close to 1.2 mm.
  2. Mesh opening: with a caliper or a magnifier with scale, measure the opening between wires at several points. Confirm that it is close to 4 mm.
  3. Spiral pitch: measure the distance covered by 10 spirals in the direction of travel and divide by 10.
  4. Loop pitch: measure the distance covered by 10 loops across the width and divide by 10.
  5. Overall thickness after rolling: with a caliper, at several points across the width.
  6. Width, length and edge finish.
Quick identification test

If there are visible connectors crossing the belt width, it is not a Z47. If there are no connectors, the surface is flat on both sides and the spirals are interlocked directly with one another, measure the wire and opening to confirm the model.

Specification Checklist

When requesting a replacement belt or a technical review, send the information below. The more complete the checklist, the lower the risk of an incorrect specification.

Belt data

  • Identified model (balanced, CB or Z47) and designation, if known
  • Overall width and overall length
  • Loop count per 12" of width and connector count per 12" of length, when applicable
  • Spiral wire and connector diameters
  • Measured mesh opening and pitches
  • Overall thickness
  • Material and edge finish
  • Type of splice used

Oven data

  • Maximum operating temperature
  • Head and tail drum diameters
  • Oven length and belt path
  • Tensioning system and tracking system
  • Product processed and load per square meter

Recommended photos

  • Top view with a ruler placed across the width
  • Top view with a ruler placed along the length
  • Close-up of the belt edge
  • Side view showing the thickness
  • Existing splice
  • Underside of the belt

Common Measuring Mistakes

Counting at a single point

Wear, repairs and local deformation change the count. Always measure at several points and use the value that repeats.

Measuring near the splice

The splice area may have a different pitch from the rest of the belt and does not represent the original weave.

Confusing spirals with loops

The width count refers to the loops along a single connector, not to the number of spirals in the belt.

Measuring the wire on the rolled face

On flattened or rolled wire, the measurement on the flat face is smaller than the original wire diameter.

Rounding the gauge without recording the measurement

Always record the measured diameter in millimeters, in addition to the estimated gauge.

Measuring length without tension

Woven belts elongate under tension. The length must reflect the operating condition in the oven.

Conclusion

Correct specification of an oven belt starts with identifying the weave and continues with measurements taken in a standardized way. On a balanced weave belt, the counts per 12" and the gauges define the construction. On a CB belt, the number of connectors inside each loop indicates the type of compound weave. On a Z47 belt, the absence of connectors, the rolled surface, the wire diameter and the mesh opening confirm the model.

With this information recorded, the replacement belt can be specified with confidence, reducing start-up adjustments and preserving the behavior of the oven.
